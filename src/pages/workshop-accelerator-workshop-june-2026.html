Accelerator Workshop June 2026

Norfolk, United Kingdom · 15–26 June 2026

This workshop has ended.

Where regenerative farming meets rewilding

In June 2026 the Soil Food Web Foundation returned to Wild Ken Hill, a 4,000-acre regenerative farm and rewilding estate on the Norfolk coast, about three hours from London (UK Accelerator Workshop page). It was our second workshop there, one year after June 2025 (SFW workshops page). All 25 places sold out (June 2026 workshop page).

Wild Ken Hill uses rewilding, regenerative farming and traditional conservation side by side. Its land stretches from the sea through coastal scrub, freshwater marshes, heathland and wood pasture to nature-rich farmland, and more than 2,500 species have been recorded there (UK Accelerator Workshop page, FAQ). The estate also runs Soil Food Web based composting on its farmland (UK Accelerator Workshop page), so students learned on land where the method is already at work.

What the workshop was for

The Accelerator Workshop is the fastest way through the hands-on part of our advanced training. What often takes students 18 to 24 months, building multiple compost piles alone, they did here in two weeks, in a focused and supportive environment (UK Accelerator Workshop page). The ten days covered all the learning outcomes for the BioComplete™ Compost Production and Biological Liquid Amendments courses (UK Accelerator Workshop page). Students who attended all ten days, completed the four Foundation Courses and the Soil Microscopy Course, and submitted their learning objectives document could graduate both courses (UK Accelerator Workshop page).

The workshop was open to all current SFW students who had completed Foundation Course 3 and watched the lectures in Foundation Course 4, plus an online pre-workshop training course (UK Accelerator Workshop page). That preparation meant students arrived ready to work.

What students did

Students worked in teams of four or five, each with its own instructor who reviewed their recipes and followed the trajectory of every pile with them (UK Accelerator Workshop page, FAQ). Over ten days the teams built eight compost piles by hand in a barn on the estate (Linnea Moritz, attended the workshop). Each team chose its own ingredients and wrote its own recipes, then monitored, turned and charted its piles for the rest of the workshop (Linnea Moritz, attended the workshop). With eight piles side by side, every student could see how different recipes, feedstocks and management styles played out in real time (Linnea Moritz, attended the workshop; UK Accelerator Workshop page).

The workshop was built so students would engage all their senses: they see, feel, hear and smell what correct composting is like (UK Accelerator Workshop page). Over the two weeks they also:

  • Learned to source and store compost materials under different constraints (UK Accelerator Workshop page)
  • Organized group compost building and turning by hand, which builds leadership skills (UK Accelerator Workshop page)
  • Diagnosed failing piles in real time and learned how to turn one around (UK Accelerator Workshop page)
  • Made compost extracts with several methods and brewed compost tea at small and large scale (UK Accelerator Workshop page)
  • Practised several field application methods for liquid amendments (UK Accelerator Workshop page)
  • Ran a nematode extraction from the materials they had helped produce, and made a protozoan infusion from local materials (UK Accelerator Workshop page)
  • Plotted a sampling grid to practise large-scale field work (UK Accelerator Workshop page)
  • Used applied microscopy on soil, compost, extracts, teas, nematode extractions and protozoan infusions, with an expert beside them (UK Accelerator Workshop page)

The training team brought a wide mix of skills, including farming, biology, agronomy and permaculture (UK Accelerator Workshop page).

A pile for Dr. Elaine

This was the first workshop at Wild Ken Hill after the death of the school's founder, Dr. Elaine Ingham, on 16 February 2026 (Boston Globe). One of the eight piles was built as a tribute to her, and the finished pile was covered in flowers in her memory (Linnea Moritz, attended the workshop). Students carried on the work she started, with their own hands, in her honour.

Why it matters

England has set itself a deadline for its soils. The government's revised Environmental Improvement Plan aims to bring at least 40 percent of England's agricultural soil into sustainable management by 2028, and 60 percent by 2030 (AHDB). Farmers are already being paid to start: by October 2025, soil management actions under the Sustainable Farming Incentive covered 44 percent of England's farmed area (Defra). Targets like these need people who can read what is happening in a soil and know how to bring it back to life. That is the skill the Accelerator Workshop teaches.

Wild Ken Hill shows that farming and wildlife can recover on the same land. Since 2019 the estate has rewilded about 1,000 acres of poor-quality farmland, and within three years the average number of plant species in its survey plots roughly doubled, from 16.8 to 33.2 (Rewilding Britain; BirdGuides). The farmed land is thriving too. In a 2024 survey, the estate's regenerative farm plots held an average of 292 invertebrate species each, against 241 in the rewilding area (Wild Ken Hill).

The workshop's impact also travels. Farm manager Nick Padwick, who hosts the students, took the Soil Food Web approach to Pakistan through his company Wild Soils UK: Soil Health Week in September 2025 reached more than 600 participants across 60 districts (Soil Food Web Foundation). Students who train at Wild Ken Hill learn from someone who teaches the method on several continents. And many workshop groups keep supporting each other years after the workshop ends (UK Accelerator Workshop page), so every cohort adds to a growing network of people who know how to bring soil back to life.
