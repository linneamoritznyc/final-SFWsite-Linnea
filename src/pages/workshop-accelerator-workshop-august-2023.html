Accelerator Workshop August 2023

Área Metropolitana de San José, Costa Rica · 7–18 August 2023

This workshop has ended.

Where the Costa Rica workshops began

In August 2023, the Soil Food Web School brought its ten-day Accelerator Workshop to the metro area around San José (workshop page; SFW workshops page). It is the earliest Costa Rica workshop on the school's list (SFW workshops page), and the first of five Costa Rica workshops held or scheduled between 2023 and 2027 (SFW workshops page).

San José sits in the Central Valley, home to about seven in ten Costa Ricans (Britannica). The valley's soils formed from the volcanic material and lava of four nearby volcanoes and are naturally rich (Britannica). Coffee farms replaced the valley's forests around 1850, and coffee growing since then has leached the soils and reduced their fertility (Britannica). It is a place where the soil once had everything, and where bringing its life back makes a real difference.

What the workshop was for

The workshop trained students from the school's consultant and grower training programs to make biological amendments by hand, working alongside the Soil Food Web team for ten days (workshop page). Biological amendments such as compost return organic matter and active microbial life to soil, and the evidence for what that does is strong. In a 2025 study of 56 farmer-managed fields, fields treated with compost scored 31 percent higher on soil health than reference fields (Edlinger et al., 2025). Their soils showed 45 percent more microbial respiration, a measure of how active soil life is, and 42 percent more capacity to hold on to nutrients, while wheat yields held steady (Edlinger et al., 2025).

Why it matters

Costa Rica's food system leans heavily on imports. In 2021 the country imported about 99 percent of the maize it ate, 92 percent of its beans and 69 percent of its rice, which places it among the most import-dependent countries in the world for basic grains (Semanario Universidad). Farmers also depend on imported fertilizer. When Russia's invasion of Ukraine sent fertilizer prices sharply higher in 2022, farmers across Central America saw their production costs spike, wrote former Costa Rican president Carlos Alvarado Quesada (Fortune).

The soils that feed the country are under strain. The slopes of the Irazú volcano in Cartago, just east of San José, grow about 80 percent of Costa Rica's vegetables (Semanario Universidad). Researchers measured erosion there at an average of 50 tonnes of soil per hectare per year, and up to 90 tonnes in years of extreme rain (Semanario Universidad). University of Costa Rica geographer Víctor Cortés says farmers in the area rely more and more on machinery and fertilizer as the land's productivity falls (Semanario Universidad).

Farmers in Costa Rica are already looking for another way. About 5,500 farmers have learned to make their own biological inputs from farm waste and forest soil microbes, through training run by the Ministry of Agriculture, the national training institute INA and the state distance university UNED (AmeliaRueda.com). Some cut their agrochemical costs by more than 40 percent (AmeliaRueda.com). In September 2026 the Ministry of Agriculture launched a national bioinputs strategy for 2026 to 2035, with training for producers and extension workers as one of its four main lines of work (La Región). At the same time, certified organic farmland fell 8.6 percent between 2024 and 2025, according to state plant health data cited by the ecological federation Fecon (Delfino.cr). Programs like these need skilled local people who can teach soil biology. This workshop started a run of Costa Rica workshops that is building a local network of consultants and growers who can carry that knowledge to farms (SFW workshops page).
