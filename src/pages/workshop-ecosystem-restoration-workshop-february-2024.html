Ecosystem Restoration Workshop February 2024

Yucatán, México · 4–10 February 2024

This workshop has ended.

From cattle ranch back to forest

In February 2024 the Soil Food Web School ran a six-day, in-person Ecosystem Restoration Workshop in Mexico with Ecosystem Restoration Communities (Beneath the Surface newsletter; Yucatán workshop page). The training was first planned by the Ecosystem Restoration Foundation, and the two organizations agreed that the school would host it instead (Enrollment Agreement, Mexico 2024).

It took place at the Earth Connection Center Biological Reserve, 39 hectares of dry tropical forest on the Yucatán Peninsula, with a cenote and Mayan ruins on the land (Yucatán workshop page). For many years the forest had been clear cut and used as a cattle ranch, which left the land exposed and heavily degraded (Yucatán workshop page). Canadian ecologist Paul Morris and Mexican agronomist Sophia Ortiz founded the reserve in 2015 to restore it (Climate Cleanup).

The land has answered. Since restoration began in 2016, the soil has turned brown, an organic layer has developed, and the tree canopy has started to close, so the soil holds more moisture (Yucatán workshop page). Wildlife has returned in greater diversity and numbers, and the reserve's growing species list is one of its proudest achievements (Yucatán workshop page). The team plants native species, is building a food forest and is testing other regenerative ways to grow food (Yucatán workshop page). Their goal is to share what works with local Mayan communities and help restore thousands of hectares (Yucatán workshop page).

What the workshop was for

The workshop was designed for students of the Introduction to Ecosystem Restoration course (Yucatán workshop page), which the school created together with Ecosystem Restoration Communities (Explorer.land). It gave them the chance to put the online course into practice with their own hands and to write a restoration plan for a small plot of land on site (Enrollment Agreement, Mexico 2024). The aim was to give students the confidence, connections and know-how to take what they learned out into the world (Yucatán workshop page).

What students did

Paul and Sophia took students through the steps of running a restoration project that can grow (Yucatán workshop page). Over six days, participants:

  • Assessed a healthy reference ecosystem (Yucatán workshop page)
  • Collected soil samples and other data to assess the health of a site (Yucatán workshop page)
  • Practised identifying species (Yucatán workshop page)
  • Learned tree and plant nursery skills (Yucatán workshop page)
  • Built habitat structures to increase biodiversity (Yucatán workshop page)
  • Wrote a restoration plan for a real site (Yucatán workshop page)

They also discussed how to remove the pressures that degrade an ecosystem, how to raise money for restoration work and how to involve the local community (Yucatán workshop page).

The team

Paul Morris has more than 25 years of experience in ecology, botany, ecosystem restoration and regenerative farming, and is a Certified Ecological Restoration Practitioner with the Society for Ecological Restoration (Yucatán workshop page). Sophia Ortiz is an agronomy engineer who works with students on soil regeneration, local plant propagation and regenerative agriculture, and is developing a botanical garden of local species (Yucatán workshop page). Ashleigh Brown, co-founder and education coordinator at Ecosystem Restoration Communities and producer of the Introduction to Ecosystem Restoration course, supported the week and documented it so other restoration communities around the world could offer similar workshops (Yucatán workshop page).

Why it matters

The forests of the Yucatán are disappearing fast. The peninsula lost 285,580 hectares of forest between 2019 and 2023, about 196 hectares a day, driven by agribusiness, cattle ranching, industrial pig and poultry farms, tourism and the Tren Maya railway (Excélsior). Once the forest is gone, the soil goes with it. Most of Yucatán state has shallow, stony soils, and forming new soil takes hundreds to thousands of years (CICY).

What happens to the soil also reaches the water. The people of the Yucatán drink from an aquifer reached through cenotes, and a 2025 study of ten cenotes found PFAS chemicals in eight, elevated E. coli in five and high nitrate levels in two (Kopczynski et al., 2025). Restoration that rebuilds living soil helps keep water and nutrients in the ground: soils treated with compost hold more nutrients and support more active microbial life (Edlinger et al., 2025).

The region also holds one of the world's oldest models of farming with the forest. The Maya milpa has been managed in rotation for 3,500 years, with long fallows that let the forest grow back between plantings (UNDP), and in 2023 FAO recognized it as a Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System (El Financiero). This workshop connected that heritage to a global movement. Ecosystem Restoration Communities is an official partner of the UN Decade on Ecosystem Restoration (Yucatán workshop page). At the time of the workshop its network had more than 60 restoration initiatives (Yucatán workshop page), and it now counts 86 initiatives in 39 countries, caring for more than 103,000 hectares of recovering land (Resilience.org).
