How it works

How the soil food web works

Healthy soil is a living system. Billions of organisms, bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, and microarthropods, form a web of relationships with plants that feeds them, builds the soil around them, and protects them. Here's how it works.

{{img src="https://new.soilfoodweb.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Screenshot-2026-09-17-172507.jpg" eager}}

The soil food web

Plants may feed us but can't produce all the nutrition they need on their own. Through their roots, they release sugars and other compounds that attract and feed a community of microorganisms, and in return, that community delivers nutrients, builds structure, and defends its host. This web of eating and being eaten is what makes soil function.

Nutrient cycling

A plant invests 30–40% of the sugars it makes through photosynthesis into the soil to feed microbes. Bacteria and fungi produce enzymes that break down minerals and organic matter, unlocking the nutrients they contain. When protozoa and nematodes consume those bacteria and fungi, the surplus is released right at the root, in plant-available form. The plant controls the exchange, drawing what it needs, when it needs it.

Source for "30–40% of the sugars". This is Dr. Ingham's teaching figure. This repo's version says "a large share of the carbon it fixes" and cites Kuzyakov and Domanski (2000), Journal of Plant Nutrition and Soil Science 163: 421–431. Evan or Carla Portugal picks one.

Building soil structure

Beneficial bacteria produce glues that bind soil particles into microaggregates; fungal hyphae weave those into larger macroaggregates. The result behaves like a sponge: holding water for extended periods, letting air flow in, resisting erosion by wind and rain, and reducing the need for irrigation.

Suppressing weeds

Beneficial fungi secrete enzymes that gradually acidify their surroundings, slowing the bacteria that convert nitrogen into nitrate, the form many fast-growing weeds depend on. As the fungal community establishes, conditions shift to favor the plants you're growing.

{{video id="372479571" h="4cced96d80" thumb="https://new.soilfoodweb.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/7.jpg" title="Suppressing weeds (animation)" alt=""}}

Protecting plants from pests and disease

Most disease-causing organisms do best where oxygen is scarce. Below ground, well-structured soil keeps the root zone oxygen-rich, favoring the beneficial organisms that compete with, inhibit, and prey on pathogens. Above ground, covering leaves and stems with beneficial microorganisms, applied as compost extracts and teas, fills the niches that pests and diseases would otherwise occupy. A surface already inhabited leaves little room for invaders.

{{video id="372480255" h="7c04397066" thumb="https://new.soilfoodweb.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Video-Thumbnails-5.jpg" title="Protecting plants from pests and disease (animation)" alt=""}}

Sequestering carbon, avoiding emissions

Plants pull carbon from the atmosphere and invest much of it into the soil, where it is built into microbial bodies, soil aggregates, and long-lived fungal networks. Biological management also avoids emissions at the source: less synthetic fertilizer to manufacture and transport, and fewer fuel-intensive passes over the field.
