Accelerator Workshop February 2026

Santa Fe, United States · 23 Feb – 6 Mar 2026

This workshop has ended.

Living soil in the high desert

The February 2026 Accelerator Workshop took place at Synergia Ranch, about 15 miles south of Santa Fe, New Mexico (Wikipedia). It was our dryland workshop, held where water is scarce and every bit of soil organic matter counts.

The ranch has a remarkable history. It was founded in 1969 by John P. Allen, who in 1984 launched the Biosphere 2 project with Ed Bass (Wikipedia). The ranch covers 111 acres, and its orchard grows on high-desert clay at about 6,300 feet; since 2019 the farm has used a no-till approach (Institute of Ecotechnics). When the orchard was planted, a compost pit three feet wide and three feet deep was dug under every tree (Institute of Ecotechnics). Decades of that care show in the soil: recent tests found 4.5 to 5.5 percent organic matter in the farm's soils, against 1.3 percent in soils nearby (Institute of Ecotechnics). Students learned to build soil life on a farm that has already shown how much it can change.

What students did

The Accelerator Workshop gives students the experience of building and managing multiple compost piles in two weeks, work that often takes 18 to 24 months alone (UK Accelerator Workshop page). In the standard format, students work in small teams with a mentor, build BioComplete™ compost piles from scratch, monitor and turn them, make compost extracts and teas, run nematode extractions and protozoan infusions, and assess everything they make under the microscope (UK Accelerator Workshop page). Together these cover the learning outcomes for the BioComplete™ Compost Production and Biological Liquid Amendments courses (UK Accelerator Workshop page).

The first workshop without Dr. Elaine

The workshop opened on 23 February, one week after Dr. Elaine Ingham died on 16 February 2026 (Boston Globe). It was the first workshop without the school's founder, and students carried on the work she started: building living compost, brewing extracts and teas, and studying soil life at the microscope.

Why it matters

This region is running short of water. The southwestern megadrought from 2000 to 2021 was the driest 22-year period in the region since at least the year 800, and climate change accounts for about 42 percent of its soil moisture deficit (Columbia Climate School). In July 2022 the Rio Grande ran dry in Albuquerque for the first time in 40 years (Smithsonian Magazine).

In dry country, soil organic matter works like a reservoir. Each 1 percent increase in organic matter lets the top foot of soil hold about 3,400 more gallons of plant-available water per acre (University of Nebraska-Lincoln Water). By that rule of thumb, the gap between Synergia's soils and its neighbors' is worth thousands of extra gallons of water per acre (Institute of Ecotechnics; University of Nebraska-Lincoln Water). Living soil also holds itself in place: US Geological Survey research on the Colorado Plateau finds that dust emissions rise during drought, and that biological soil covers are critical to keeping desert soils stable (USGS).

The need on the food side is just as clear. About one in six New Mexicans faces food insecurity, and the state has the fourth highest rate of child food insecurity in the country (Roadrunner Food Bank). New Mexico is investing in the answer: its Healthy Soil Program has awarded more than 2.4 million dollars to 158 on-the-ground projects (New Mexico Department of Agriculture), and its principles include maximizing biodiversity and minimizing external inputs (NMDA). Every grant like that works better with someone on the ground who knows how to build soil biology. Each graduate of this workshop is one more of those people. We return to New Mexico in May 2027.
