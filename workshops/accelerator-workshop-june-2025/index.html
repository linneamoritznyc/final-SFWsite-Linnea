Accelerator Workshop June 2025

Norfolk, United Kingdom · 16–27 June 2025

This workshop has ended.

A year's worth of experience in ten days

In June 2025 the Soil Food Web School brought its Accelerator Workshop to Wild Ken Hill, a 4,000-acre regenerative farm and rewilding estate on the Norfolk coast (UK Accelerator Workshop page; Wild Ken Hill). It was the school's first workshop there (SFW workshops page). For ten days, students worked side by side with SFW mentors to build, monitor and troubleshoot thermophilic compost piles, brew biological liquid amendments and study everything they made under the microscope (Workshop Handbook, June 2025; UK Accelerator Workshop page).

Most of our students learn online and then build their first compost piles alone at home. That path works, and it is slow. What often takes students 18 to 24 months on their own, they did here in two weeks, in a focused and supportive setting with experts from across the globe there to guide them (UK Accelerator Workshop page).

What the workshop was for

The Accelerator Workshop is an alternative, but equivalent, learning pathway through the hands-on stages of our training (Enrollment Agreement, June 2025). Everything in the workshop was built around the learning outcomes of what are now the BioComplete™ Compost Production and Biological Liquid Amendments courses (Workshop Handbook, June 2025; UK Accelerator Workshop page, FAQ). Students who attended all ten days, and who completed their Foundation Courses and microscopy training, could graduate both (Enrollment Agreement, June 2025).

It is designed for people who learn best in person (Workshop Handbook, June 2025). Students learn through their senses. They see, feel, hear and smell what good composting is like (UK Accelerator Workshop page), and they learn it with their hands.

What students did

Students worked in five small teams, each with mentors (Workshop Handbook, June 2025). The plan was for the group to build six compost piles from scratch and manage them all at the same time (Workshop Handbook, June 2025). Some piles were designed to succeed and others were designed to fail, so students could understand the principles at play and practise rescuing a pile that was going wrong (Workshop Handbook, June 2025). Every morning and afternoon they monitored temperature and moisture and decided when to turn (Workshop Handbook, June 2025), and they charted the results like a working consultant (UK Accelerator Workshop page).

Alongside the piles, students:

  • Made compost extracts and brewed compost teas, then practised applying them in the field (Workshop Handbook, June 2025; UK Accelerator Workshop page)
  • Ran nematode extractions from the materials they had helped produce (UK Accelerator Workshop page; Workshop Handbook, June 2025)
  • Started protozoan infusions from local materials (UK Accelerator Workshop page; Workshop Handbook, June 2025)
  • Used applied microscopy at key stages to assess soil, compost and liquid amendments, with an expert beside them to help identify what they were seeing (Workshop Handbook, June 2025; UK Accelerator Workshop page)
  • Learned soil sampling and field assessment, and how to turn their findings into a client proposal (Workshop Handbook, June 2025)
  • Studied vermicomposting and large-scale composting (Workshop Handbook, June 2025)

The handbook warned students that the schedule would change almost every day, because nobody can predict exactly when a pile will heat up (Workshop Handbook, June 2025). Learning to work with that is part of the training. June in Norfolk gave them about 17 hours of daylight a day to do it (Workshop Handbook, June 2025). The final day was planned around a certificate ceremony (Workshop Handbook, June 2025).

Why Wild Ken Hill

Wild Ken Hill runs rewilding, regenerative farming and traditional conservation side by side. Its land stretches from the sea through coastal scrub, freshwater marshes, heathland and wood pasture to nature-rich farmland, and more than 2,500 species have been recorded there (Workshop Handbook, June 2025; UK Accelerator Workshop page, FAQ).

It is also a working example of what students came to learn. Farm manager Nick Padwick became the first British farmer to qualify as a Soil Food Web consultant, one of just 39 worldwide at the time (Wild Ken Hill; Wild Ken Hill). He now makes about 750 tons of compost a year (October 2025 newsletter), and compost has gone onto all of the estate's farmland (Wild Ken Hill). In one year his soil tests moved from a fungal-to-bacterial ratio of 0.01 to 0.6, heading toward the 1 to 1 balance he is aiming for (Farmers Weekly). The farm has used no insecticide for over a decade and is converting its arable land to organic (Wild Ken Hill). In 2022 an independent assessment placed it in the top 5 percent of UK arable farms for carbon sequestration (Wild Ken Hill).

Students also had field instruction on Nick's farm and saw the same process working at farm scale (October 2025 newsletter).

Why it matters

British farming relies heavily on bought-in nitrogen. The UK imports around 60 percent of the nitrogen fertilizer it uses (AHDB), and the price of ammonium nitrate jumped from about £395 a tonne in 2021 to £650 to £850 in 2022 (AHDB; AHDB). Much of the excess washes into rivers: in the catchment of Norfolk's River Wensum, 40 percent of water bodies are at risk of exceeding the drinking water limit for nitrate (University of East Anglia). Meanwhile, arable soils in England and Wales have lost 40 to 60 percent of their organic carbon (Environment Agency). A farmer-led survey of over 1,300 hectares found that 42 percent of fields may be over-worked, judged by missing or rare earthworms (Stroud, 2019).

Compost is one of the most reliable ways to rebuild that carbon: a global analysis of 1,972 comparisons found organic amendments raise cropland soil organic carbon by 26.9 percent on average (Bai et al., 2023). Rebuilding the life in the soil answers several of these problems at once, and it spreads as fast as there are people who know how to do it. Every student who leaves an Accelerator Workshop has worked through all the learning outcomes for BioComplete™ Compost Production and Biological Liquid Amendments (UK Accelerator Workshop page), so they go home able to make biologically complete compost and liquid amendments, read what is happening under a microscope, and help the farms and gardens around them. Many groups keep supporting each other years after the workshop ends (UK Accelerator Workshop page), which means the network of skilled practitioners keeps growing long after the ten days are over.

Another student from this workshop wrote: "The whole workshop was incredible. Having the vast experience of so many mentors and the variety of protocols we covered was amazing and to be surrounded by likeminded individuals that were passionate about the SFW made it an life changing experience" (June 2025 student, quoted on the UK Accelerator Workshop page).

"Fantastic way to gain a huge amount of experience with different piles, in a very short amount of time… great deal of experience regarding compost pile creation. Experimentation ideas from the group as a collective. Networking with my peers and increased confidence and understanding."

Melita Coggins, June 2025 student (UK Accelerator Workshop page)
