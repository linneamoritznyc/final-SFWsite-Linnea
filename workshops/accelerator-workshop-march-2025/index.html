Accelerator Workshop March 2025

Área Metropolitana de San José, Costa Rica · 3–14 March 2025

This workshop has ended.

Ten days that work like a year

In March 2025 the Soil Food Web School held a ten-day, in-person Accelerator Workshop near San José, Costa Rica (Enrollment Agreement, March 2025). It was designed so that students could complete the hands-on stages of our advanced training through an alternative, but equivalent, learning pathway (Enrollment Agreement, March 2025). Those stages are now the BioComplete™ Compost Production and Biological Liquid Amendments courses (UK Accelerator Workshop page, FAQ). Students who attended all ten days, and who completed their Foundation Courses and microscopy training, could graduate both (Enrollment Agreement, March 2025).

The format is made for people who learn best in person (Workshop Handbook, March 2025). One student from this workshop summed it up: "Super helpful, super fun, such a great group of people!" (March 2025 student, quoted on the UK Accelerator Workshop page).

Where students learned

The host was Armonía Agricultura Orgánica in La Asunción de Belén, Heredia, 11 km north of San José in the Greater Metropolitan Area (Workshop Handbook, March 2025). Armonía's mission is to become an example project of organic product management, showing ecological responsibility and supplying locally grown food (Workshop Handbook, March 2025). Students worked in covered and open-air workspaces, with indoor classrooms for microscopy (Workshop Handbook, March 2025). March falls in the dry season (Workshop Handbook, March 2025).

What students did

The group built five compost piles over the ten days and managed them all at the same time (Workshop Handbook, March 2025). Some were designed to succeed and others were designed to fail, so students could understand the principles and mechanics at play (Workshop Handbook, March 2025). From day three, they monitored every pile each morning and afternoon and turned it when needed (Workshop Handbook, March 2025).

The ten days followed a clear arc (Workshop Handbook, March 2025). Students learned the feedstock categories, wrote their compost recipes and built their first pile in the opening days, then took soil samples and started microscopy of soil and compost (Workshop Handbook, March 2025). The second week moved to compost teas, nematode extraction, protozoan infusions and humic acid extraction, with a certificate ceremony planned for the final day (Workshop Handbook, March 2025). Applied microscopy ran through it all, so students assessed their compost and liquid amendments at key stages (Workshop Handbook, March 2025).

Mentor Gerald Ramírez demonstrated how to make liquid amendments, and students and mentors practised microscopy together (2025 in Review). The group also "came up with some great techniques for mixing compost materials" (October 2025 newsletter).

What students said

Students from this workshop described the value of a small team with its own mentor, and of hearing how mentors work in very different environments. One wrote: "Great to hear the contrasting applications from all the mentors and environments they are in. Also their specific points in feedstocks and recipe builds, moisture etc. Way beyond the chatting through a worksheet in google drive with the mentor… Good to have your crew in a small team with dedicated mentor" (March 2025 student, quoted on the UK Accelerator Workshop page).

Another wrote: "The mentors and students were all interesting in their diversity of backgrounds and great people in general, to meet….. SFW people are the best people!" (March 2025 student, quoted on the UK Accelerator Workshop page).

"Be prepared to work hard, listen, and watch everything very intently! Taking good notes helps tremendously. You will meet some awesome people and make new friends for life."

Donell Frank, March 2025 student (UK Accelerator Workshop page)

Why it matters

Costa Rican farms are some of the most input-intensive in the world. In 2022 the country used about 899 kg of fertilizer per hectare of arable land, close to seven times the world average of 135 kg (World Bank). Every bag is imported. When prices jumped 186 percent in 2022, the Minister of Agriculture said the country depends 100 percent on fertilizer imports, and farmers warned that for some crops fertilizer made up as much as 70 percent of production costs (Semanario Universidad).

Costa Rican farmers already have a tradition of working with soil microbes. Many collect "microorganismos de montaña," beneficial microbes from the forest floor, and multiply them on the farm; the Ministry of Agriculture describes them as microorganisms that improve soil health and plant growth (Ministerio de Agricultura y Ganadería). Researchers at the University of Costa Rica have measured these preparations and found microbial counts of a million to a hundred million colony-forming units per milliliter (Castro-Barquero & González-Acuña, 2021). The Accelerator Workshop builds on that local knowledge with the tools to see which organisms are present under a microscope, and to make compost that carries a full range of soil life. Every graduate can bring that skill to farms that are paying more and more for imported fertilizer.
