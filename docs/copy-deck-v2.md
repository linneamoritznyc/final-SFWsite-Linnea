## 13. Science, `/science`

**Title:** How the soil food web works

**H1:** How the soil food web works

**Lede:** Healthy soil is a living system. Billions of organisms form a web of relationships with plants that feeds them, builds the soil around them, and protects them. Six mechanisms, each with a drawn illustration and, where we have it, our own microscopy.

1. **The web itself.** Plants don't feed alone. Through their roots they release sugars and other compounds that attract and feed a community of microorganisms; in return that community delivers nutrients, builds structure and defends its host. This web of eating and being eaten is what makes soil function.
2. **Nutrient cycling.** A plant invests 30 to 40 percent of the sugars it makes into the soil to feed microbes. Bacteria and fungi produce enzymes that break down minerals and organic matter. When protozoa and nematodes consume those bacteria and fungi, the surplus is released at the root in plant-available form. The plant draws what it needs, when it needs it. [SOURCE: Ingham et al. 1985; USDA Soil Biology Primer 1999.]
3. **Building structure.** Bacteria produce glues that bind particles into microaggregates; fungal hyphae weave those into macroaggregates. The result behaves like a sponge: holding water, letting air in, resisting erosion.
4. **Suppressing weeds.** Beneficial fungi gradually acidify their surroundings, slowing the bacteria that convert nitrogen to nitrate, the form many fast-growing weeds depend on. As the fungal community establishes, conditions shift to favor the plants you're growing.
5. **Protecting plants.** Most disease organisms do best where oxygen is scarce. Well-structured soil keeps the root zone oxygen-rich, favoring the organisms that compete with, inhibit and prey on pathogens. Above ground, beneficial microorganisms applied as extracts and teas occupy the surfaces pests would otherwise colonize.
6. **Storing carbon.** Plants pull carbon from the air and invest much of it in the soil, where it is built into microbial bodies, aggregates and long-lived fungal networks. Biological management also avoids emissions: less fertilizer manufactured and moved, fewer passes over the field.

**On claims.** Each mechanism above is established soil biology. How much of each benefit a given field sees depends on climate, crop, starting condition and management. We publish results by site, with the person and the year, and say what a single site cannot prove. Read the case studies with that in mind.
