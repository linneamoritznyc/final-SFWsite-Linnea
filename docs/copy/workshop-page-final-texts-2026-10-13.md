## June 2026, Wild Ken Hill, UK

In June 2026, we returned to Wild Ken Hill for our second Accelerator Workshop on the Norfolk coast, and all 25 places sold out. The estate runs from the sea through coastal scrub, freshwater marshes, heathland and wood pasture to farmland, and more than 2,500 species have been recorded there. Its farmland already runs on Soil Food Web based composting, so students learned on land where the method is at work.

This year the group built and monitored eight compost piles. Students worked in teams of four or five, each with its own instructor who reviewed their recipes and followed every pile with them. With eight piles side by side, everyone could watch how different feedstocks, recipes and management choices played out in real time. When a pile started to fail, students diagnosed it on the spot and learned how to turn it around.

Building and turning the piles by hand took real teamwork, and it built leadership along the way. Students also made compost extracts with several methods, brewed compost tea at small and large scale, and practised applying liquid amendments in the field. They plotted a sampling grid for large-scale field work, ran nematode extractions from the materials they had helped produce, and studied it all under the microscope with an expert beside them. The training team brought farming, biology, agronomy and permaculture to the same compost yard.

Students come from all over the world and take what they learn back to their own corner of the globe. From Wild Ken Hill, they took home the experience of eight piles in ten days.

## February 2026, Synergia Ranch, Santa Fe, US

In February 2026, we held our Accelerator Workshop at Synergia Ranch, about 15 miles south of Santa Fe, New Mexico. It was our dryland workshop, high in the desert at about 6,300 feet, where water is scarce and every bit of soil organic matter counts.

Synergia has a remarkable story. John P. Allen founded the ranch in 1969, and in 1984 he launched the Biosphere 2 project with Ed Bass. When the orchard was planted, a compost pit three feet wide and three feet deep was dug under every tree, and since 2019 the farm has used a no-till approach. Decades of that care show in the soil. Recent tests found 4.5 to 5.5 percent organic matter in the ranch's soils, three to four times the 1.3 percent measured in soils nearby.

That made Synergia a living example of what students came to learn. The Accelerator Workshop packs the hands-on part of our advanced training into ten days: building BioComplete™ compost piles from scratch in small teams with a mentor, monitoring and turning them, making compost extracts and teas, and checking the results under the microscope. Together, that work covers the learning outcomes of our BioComplete™ Compost Production and Biological Liquid Amendments courses.

Students come from all over the world and take what they learn back to their own corner of the globe. At Synergia, they learned it on a farm that shows how far patient soil care can go, even in the desert.

## October 2025, Armonía, Costa Rica

In October 2025, we returned to Armonía Agricultura Orgánica in Heredia, Costa Rica, for our third visit in 15 months and our fourth in-person workshop of the year. Armonía is a school campus where the organic farm is the centrepiece: kids grow up knowing where their lunch comes from, and the on-site restaurant serves what the land produces.

Over ten days, students worked in small teams, each with their own mentor, building compost piles side by side. Some piles were designed to succeed and some to fail, so the group could practice rescuing a pile going wrong. Every morning and afternoon, they monitored temperature and moisture and decided when to turn, rainy season and all. What can take 18 to 24 months of solo trial and error at home, they did in two weeks.

With the piles underway, the group moved on to compost extracts and teas, nematode extractions and protozoan infusions. Students worked in open-air and covered workspaces on the farm, with indoor classrooms for microscopy, where they checked everything they made. As we like to say, you have to see, feel, hear and smell good compost to really know it.

Students come from all over the world and take what they learn back to their own corner of the globe. They left ready to produce BioComplete™ compost and biological liquid amendments, and many students keep learning from each other long after they go home.

## June 2025, Wild Ken Hill, UK

In June 2025, we brought our first Accelerator Workshop to Wild Ken Hill, a 4,000-acre regenerative farm and rewilding estate on the Norfolk coast. It is home to Nick Padwick, who has managed the estate since 2018 and was the first British farmer to qualify as a Soil Food Web consultant.

For ten days, students worked in small teams with our mentors, building compost piles and nursing them along. Some piles were designed to fail, so students could practice rescuing them. Piles heat up on their own schedule, so the timetable moved with them almost every day. Every extract, tea and sample went under the microscope.

Student Melita Coggins called it a "fantastic way to gain a huge amount of experience with different piles, in a very short amount of time."

Nick's fields were part of the classroom, and his story is worth telling. He grew up on a farm in Hampshire and has more than 30 harvests behind him. In 2009, Farmers Weekly named him both Farm Manager of the Year and Farmer of the Year. When he arrived at Ken Hill, he described the soil as "pretty dull," with hardly any life in it. In 2020 he took his first course with Dr. Elaine Ingham, and he went on to qualify as a Soil Food Web consultant, one of only 39 in the world at the time.

Today he produces around 750 tons of compost a year, and it has gone onto all of the estate's farmland. Within a year, the fungi in his soil grew from barely there to more than half the level of the bacteria, and he is aiming for an even balance. The farm has grown its crops without insecticide for more than a decade and is converting its arable land to organic. In 2022, an independent assessment placed it in the top 5 percent of UK arable farms for carbon sequestration. In his words: "We want to share what we're doing here so that we can help UK agriculture make the changes needed, at pace."

Students come from all over the world and take what they learn back to their own corner of the globe. They left Wild Ken Hill with the same skills Nick used to bring his "pretty dull" soil back to life.

## March 2025, Armonía, Costa Rica

In March 2025, we returned to Armonía Agricultura Orgánica in La Asunción de Belén, Heredia, 11 km north of San José. It was our second workshop there, eight months after the first, and this time we came in the dry season.

The heart of this workshop was the mentoring. Students worked in small teams, each with a dedicated mentor, and the group built five compost piles and ran them all at once, some designed to succeed and some to fail. From day three, they checked every pile each morning and afternoon and turned it when needed. One student wrote that it was "great to hear the contrasting applications from all the mentors and environments they are in," and that it was "good to have your crew in a small team with dedicated mentor."

Mentor Gerald Ramírez showed students how to make liquid amendments. In the second week, the group moved on to compost teas, nematode extraction, protozoan infusions and humic acid extraction. Students and mentors worked side by side at the microscopes, checking compost and amendments at each stage, and the group came up with some great techniques of their own for mixing compost materials.

Donell Frank's advice to future students captures the ten days well: "Be prepared to work hard, listen, and watch everything very intently! Taking good notes helps tremendously. You will meet some awesome people and make new friends for life."

Students come from all over the world and take what they learn back to their own corner of the globe, along with a few of those new friends.

## February 2024, Earth Connection Center, Yucatán, Mexico

In February 2024, we ran a six-day Ecosystem Restoration Workshop at the Earth Connection Center Biological Reserve on Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, together with Ecosystem Restoration Communities. The reserve covers 39 hectares of dry tropical forest, with a cenote and Mayan ruins on the land.

For many years the forest was clear cut and used as a cattle ranch. Canadian ecologist Paul Morris and Mexican agronomist Sophia Ortiz founded the reserve in 2015 to bring it back, and since restoration began in 2016, the land has responded. The soil has turned brown, an organic layer has formed, the canopy has started to close and wildlife has returned. For students, the reserve itself was the textbook.

The workshop was built for students of our Introduction to Ecosystem Restoration course, created with Ecosystem Restoration Communities, so they could put the online course into practice. Paul and Sophia led them through a restoration project step by step. Students assessed a healthy reference forest, collected soil samples, practised identifying species, learned nursery skills and built habitat structures. They also worked through the harder questions of restoration: removing the pressures that degrade land, raising money and involving the local community. Ashleigh Brown of Ecosystem Restoration Communities supported the week and documented it, so restoration communities around the world could run workshops like it.

By the end, students had written a restoration plan for a real plot on the reserve, ready to take what they learned out into the world.

## August 2023, San José area, Costa Rica

In August 2023, we brought our ten-day Accelerator Workshop to the San José area of Costa Rica for the first time. It was the start of a long relationship with the country. We have returned three times since, and another workshop is planned for January 2027.

The workshop brought together students from our consultant and grower training programs to learn how to make biological amendments by hand, working alongside the Soil Food Web team for ten days.

Biological amendments such as compost return organic matter and active microbial life to the soil, and the evidence for what that does is strong. In a 2025 study of 56 farmer-managed fields, fields treated with compost scored 31 percent higher on soil health than comparison fields. Their soil life was 45 percent more active, and their soils could hold on to 42 percent more nutrients, while wheat yields held steady.

Students come from all over the world and take what they learn back to their own corner of the globe. In 2023, that work began in Costa Rica, and it has grown with every visit since.
