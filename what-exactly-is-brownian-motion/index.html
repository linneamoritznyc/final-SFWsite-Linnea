What exactly is Brownian motion?

By Wes Sander · 2 October 2026

When we observe a sample through the microscope, with microbes and soil particles all suspended in a thin layer of water, one of the first things to stand out is how the smallest objects seem to dance with a peculiar sort of energy.

The motion looks like a vibration, and you’d think it might be easily explained by elements in the environment around the microscope, like air movement or foot traffic. But that explanation doesn’t quite seem to fit: as you can see in the video below, two bacteria in close proximity might start meandering away from one another, seeming to wander in different directions by way of their own nervous energy. Bacteria possess a wide array of motility styles, which in some cases might play a part; but this motion appears uniformly across the field of view, among bacteria and soil particles alike. In samples with greater concentrations of these small bits – those with dimensions around 1 to 5 micrometers – it can create a visual impression a bit like the static we used to see on out-of-tune television screens.

There’s a distinct explanation for this phenomenon, one that has been accepted by science for over a century, and has played a key role in the development of probability theory. Basically, when we peer at samples of soil or compost, using our relatively simple microscopes at only 400x magnification, we are witnessing direct evidence of the most basic building blocks of physical matter in the universe.

The Scottish botanist Robert Brown first documented this observable motion of small particles in 1827. Brown used a single-lens microscope to observe pollen grains on a slide that was prepared with a water suspension, just as we do with soil samples. He described the grains rupturing to spill their contents, which consisted of two particle types that today are termed ‘amyloplasts’ and ‘spherosomes.’

Both particles are close to the size of bacteria. Because they were the smallest objects he knew of, Brown called them “molecules,” using a term that had long been applied, somewhat generally, to the smallest observed (or theorized) particles of matter. This vibrating motion, Brown noted, caused the group to disperse, first near the edges, then farther inward, as enough space cleared for each particle to begin jostling about. 

Brown theorized what seemed obvious: the particles themselves were exhibiting this motion, thereby showing signs of life. He described his observations in a paper published the following year, and the phenomenon became known thereafter as “Brownian motion.” It was early in the following century when the theoretical physicist Albert Einstein contributed his own take, showing that this motion did indeed emanate from “molecules” – but in the intervening eight decades, the word had evolved to reference a far smaller thing, the group of bonded atoms that we think of today.

By statistically analyzing the movement of the particles over time, Einstein showed that the motion was coming from the water itself – it was a vibration caused by the particles of energy that comprise the atoms that form its molecules. The French physicist Jean Perrin built on this work shortly thereafter, all of which ultimately settled the question of whether atoms actually exist. Brownian motion has since become a cornerstone in the analysis of stochastic processes, allowing the modeling of an object’s movement when impacted by a set of randomly occurring factors.

In the video above, you can see various types of motion. Bacteria of several different shapes are showing high motility with different styles – long distances in straight lines, zipping back and forth, twirling in place. You can also see that jittery motion happening uniformly across all of the smallest objects, bacteria included (except those that have settled on the slide surface). The particles mostly measure around 10 or fewer micrometers in diameter. Even the one larger object, the chain of rod-shaped bacteria at center, is moving back and forth at its unanchored end, showing the effects of being buffeted on all sides by that jostling motion. And you can easily see how this general, vibration-like motion is quite different from the self-propulsion exhibited by some microbes, and from external forces like evaporation or capillary action, which would pull all objects along together in the same water current. 

In SFW work, we often stop to marvel at the hidden world we regularly find ourselves exploring through microscopy. How cool is it, then, that we get to peer even farther, into the very construction of our universe? Super cool, obviously.
